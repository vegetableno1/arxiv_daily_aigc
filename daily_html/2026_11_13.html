Diffusion models for dynamic volatility surface generation and data-driven hedging

We develop a diffusion-model framework for dynamic implied-volatility surface generation and evaluate its economic usefulness through data-driven hedging. The framework consists of two models. AD-Seq-Vol-FT further incorporates option-market structure through post-training penalties for violations of static no-arbitrage conditions. Using daily SPX option data from 2000 to 2023, we show that the proposed models generate coherent surface trajectories while capturing both cross-sectional and temporal dependence. Compared to a range of classical and data-driven benchmarks, the diffusion-based hedges maintain tracking errors near zero, substantially reduce tail risk, and exhibit particularly stable performance during the COVID-19 market disruption. 搜索能否自立标杆？环内紧缩及其在自主策略发现中的利用

Corrections for backtest overfitting such as the deflated Sharpe ratio are applied after a search ends: the searcher maximises raw performance and a separate procedure deflates the reported winner. Autonomous searches driven by large language models perform many implicit backtests before reporting, so this paper asks what changes when the correction moves inside the loop and the acceptance threshold is indexed to the running count and dispersion of the search's own trials. The threshold then becomes an object the search can optimise against: we show analytically that a naive in-loop bar admits a variance-dilution effect, in which mediocre near-duplicate candidates lower the bar because the ledger's dispersion falls faster than its trial-count factor grows. Supporting studies find that state-based stopping reaches the same outcome after 8 to 9 trials at about a twelfth of the trial budget while an adaptive budget adds nothing, that a controlled stress test reproduces the dilution effect and both defences, and that a hundred nominal trials collapse to 6 to 8 effectively independent return streams. We scope these results to the analysed system.

Market Impact Estimation via Causal Foundation Models

We present a proof-of-concept \emph{Causal Market Simulator}: a continuous-time causal prior-data fitted network (PFN) trained on a synthetic market prior in which a hidden informational flow confounds the flow--price relation by construction, with confounding strength an exact per-episode knob. On this prior, the naive estimator's bias follows a closed form we confirm empirically (up to 68\% overestimation), while the PFN tracks the unbiased true-parameter oracle at high confounding. Its \emph{do-ablated twin}, the same model with the intervention inputs removed, collapses onto the pre-window mean. Zero-shot, the model responds at the ground-truth scale to metaorders in an ABIDES limit-order book (+0.38 pre-onset standard deviations against seed-replay counterfactuals), and its intervention response replicates on about 2,300 detected flow bursts in BTC/ETH/SOL perpetuals and 3,800 on Nasdaq equities, evidence consistent with, though not proof of, causal transfer. Off-prior the response degrades gracefully, and split-conformal recalibration repairs interval calibration. Transfer hinges on encoding the metaorder as added flow rather than a clamped price level, not on more training. A recurrent state-space variant closes the remaining shape gap: its impact accrues over execution duration where the transformer's stays flat, raising the correlation with measured metaorder impact from +0.68 to between +0.92 and +0.96, at the cost of weaker on-prior deconfounding that traces to the training recipe rather than to recurrence.

Multivariate time series forecasting often improves the prediction of a target by modeling the interactions among variables, and cross-variable modules such as cross-sectional pooling, inter-variable attention, and graph structures are used to construct such interactions. However, by intervening on the cross-variable reading of existing models at inference time, we find that although these models substantially reduce forecasting error through cross-variable interaction, their reading is nearly identical for all targets: the gain appears to come from dependencies among variables but mainly comes from the common component. We term this phenomenon spurious dependency and attribute it to common-component confounding: present in every variable, the common component affects how reading distinguishes sources, so that reading tends to be shared across targets and concentrates on a few similar averages; dependencies thus make up a small share of the read-in content, and a common component composed of several drivers is only partly captured.
